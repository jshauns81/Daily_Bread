Where Daily Bread could be better

A design pass on the SwiftUI app — not a description of it. Every item is a recommendation with a proposal, ordered by how much it would change the feel of the app per unit of work.

Read from source at DailyBread/. Line references are real; open them alongside.

The thesis — two vocabularies, unevenly distributed

There are two design languages in this app, and the split is not where you'd want it. The authored language — themes, glassCard, YearHeatmapCard, AtRiskCard, ScreenTimeCard, ConfettiView, SheetKit, DayPicker — is genuinely good and specific to this product. The stock language is raw SwiftUI: 7 segmented pickers, 6 menu pickers, 7 sliders, 2 steppers, 5 date pickers, 12 lists.

The problem isn't that stock exists. It's where it sits. The authored work is concentrated in the display surfaces — the things you look at. The stock work is concentrated in the input surfaces — the things you touch. So the app looks bespoke and feels generic, and the gap is widest at exactly the moments that carry emotion: checking off a chore, logging a drive, setting what a chore is worth.

Authored — the things you look at
  • YearHeatmapCard — 365 cells, the app's best idea
  • AtRiskCard — stakes before the list
  • ScreenTimeCard — earned/spent as one object
  • ConfettiView — the day-complete moment
  • Themes — 6 palettes, whole-surface
  • SheetKit — header / card / field / action bar
  • DayPicker — 7 tappable day pips
Stock — the things you touch
  • The check button — Image(systemName:"circle")
  • Drive logging — 3 stacked DatePickers
  • Weekly target — a Stepper
  • Importance — a bare 0–10 Slider
  • Rarity / Category / Condition — menu pickers
  • Date range — 2 more DatePickers
  • Every admin form — List + Form
The one-line version: you authored the nouns and left the verbs stock. The recommendations below mostly move craft from display to input.

Recommendations

R1Author the check buttonHighest impact

TodayView.swift:354 — Image(systemName: item.isDone ? "checkmark.circle.fill" : "circle") · .foregroundStyle(item.isDone ? Color.accentColor : Color.secondary)

This is the most-tapped control in the product and the single most stock thing in it. A grey SF Symbol circle at .title2. Two problems beyond the styling: grey reads as disabled rather than as an empty vessel waiting to be filled, and completing one chore produces no feedback at all — confetti only fires when the entire day is done, so 90% of completions land silently. A kid does this six times a day for years. It should feel like something.

Proposal. A purpose-built ChoreCheck: unchecked is a 2.5px ring in label.opacity(0.22) — warm, empty, clearly waiting; checked springs to filled accent with a halo that blooms and settles. Add a rigid haptic on check and nothing on uncheck (undo shouldn't be rewarded). For earning chores, flash the ring gold for ~180ms before it settles to accent — the one moment where money and action are the same event, using the semantic colour you already reserve.

R2Drive logging should ask for duration, not three timestampsHighest impact

DrivingLogView.swift:247–249 — three DatePickers (Date, Start, End) stacked inside an authored SheetCard(title:"When")

This is the sharpest stock/authored collision in the app: your own sheet card wrapping three iOS system pickers, which makes the stock contents more conspicuous, not less. But the real problem is conceptual. The log exists to accumulate hours toward a licence. Duration is the payload; start and end are bookkeeping the teen has to do arithmetic on. Right now the number that matters is the one number the form never shows.

Proposal. Lead with the date as chips (most drives are logged same-day — Today / Yesterday / pick), then make duration the hero: large, adjustable, with preset chips for the common cases. Keep exact start/end behind a "Set exact times" disclosure for the states that require it. Night driving already auto-detects from the times — if exact times are collapsed, keep Auto but let them override, which the segmented control already allows.

R3A Stepper sits 12 lines from your own DayPickerCheap + obvious

ChoreEditorSheet.swift:195 Stepper("up to \(weeklyTarget)× a week", …) — and ChoreEditorSheet.swift:207 DayPicker(selected: $selectedDays)

Same sheet, same question ("when does this happen?"), two completely different vocabularies. You already solved this: DayPicker is seven tappable pips and it's good. The weekly-frequency branch reaches for a system Stepper instead, so the user toggling between "specific days" and "n times a week" watches the control language change underneath them.

Proposal. Reuse the DayPicker geometry for count — seven pips, tap the nth to set the target. Identical rhythm, identical hit targets, one vocabulary for scheduling. It also makes the 1–7 bound self-evident instead of enforced by an invisible in: 1...7.

R4"Importance 7" doesn't mean anything to a parentMedium

ChoreEditorSheet.swift:242 — Slider(value: $importance, in: 0...10, step: 1)

A bare 0–10 slider asks the parent to invent a scale. Is 6 a lot? What's the difference between 6 and 7? Nobody knows, including the person who set it — and this value feeds the urgency language in AtRiskCard, so an arbitrary number becomes real words on the kid's screen.

Proposal. Five named stakes, as chips. The label is the scale, and it maps directly onto the urgency copy already written. Keep the 0–10 storage; change only the input.

R5Seven different opacities all mean "off"Systemic

Across 8 files: Color.secondary.opacity( 0.06 · 0.12 · 0.13 · 0.14 · 0.16 · 0.3 · 0.35 — CalendarView:142, PlannerGridView:126, PlannerView:480, SheetKit:164, ScreenTimeSettingsSheet:115, ApprovalsView:419

These are all the same intent — the unselected/inactive fill behind a toggle, pip, or chip — written seven ways. Individually invisible; collectively it's why two adjacent controls can look subtly mismatched, and it's the kind of thing that drifts further with every new screen.

Proposal. Two named tokens on DB — fillOff (~0.12) for control backgrounds and fillSubtle (~0.06) for large surfaces — and one fillStrong (~0.32) for the pressed/prominent case. Three values, named, done. This is a 30-minute change that permanently removes a class of inconsistency.

R6Rarity is a semantic colour riding on a themeable oneInvariant leak

AchievementsView.swift:180 — case "rare": return .accentColor

You treat gold and Help-red as invariants that survive every theme, correctly. Rarity is the same kind of thing — "rare" should mean one colour everywhere, the way it does in every game a kid has played. But rare resolves to the theme accent, so rare is raspberry in Sunroom, blue in Sky and pink in Rosewater. Worse, in Sky it becomes indistinguishable from every ordinary accent control on the screen, so the badge stops signalling anything.

Proposal. Move the four rarities into the invariant set alongside gold and help — fixed hues, tuned once for legibility on both light and dark grounds. Rarity is information, not decoration.

R7One hardcoded hue in the whole app — and it's indigoSmall

DrivingLogView.swift:147 — .foregroundStyle(entry.isNightDriving ? Color.indigo : Color.accentColor)

Theme discipline across this codebase is genuinely strong; this is nearly the only hue that escapes it. Indigo is a reasonable instinct for "night", but it's unowned — it belongs to no theme and will clash somewhere. If night driving needs its own colour, it's an invariant and should join the set in R6. If it doesn't, use DB.secondary.

R8Menu pickers hide information you've already designedMedium

AchievementDefinitionsView.swift:249–266 — .pickerStyle(.menu) for Rarity, Category, Condition · DrivingLogView:253 Weather

Rarity has a full visual identity — colour, weight, meaning — and the editor reduces it to a grey menu row reading "Rare ⌄". The parent authoring an achievement can't see what they're choosing. Weather is four options with obvious glyphs, also behind a menu.

Proposal. Where the option set is small and visually coded, show the options: rarity as its four coloured chips, weather as its four SF Symbols. Keep .menu only for genuinely long lists (Category, Which chore, Before-hour). Rule of thumb — if the choice has a colour or a glyph, don't hide it in a menu.

R9Lists you're already fightingStructural

TodayView.swift:165–210 — every Section carries .listRowBackground(Color.clear) + custom listRowInsets

On the card-heavy screens, List is being neutralised row by row so authored cards can sit inside it. You're paying for List's chrome and then deleting it. But not everywhere: Today's chore rows use swipeActions for Done and Help, which is real, native and worth keeping — don't throw List out there.

Proposal. Split by need. Screens whose rows are all clear-backgrounded cards with no swipe (Earnings, Awards, the heatmap/screen-time stacks) → ScrollView + LazyVStack with your own spacing. Screens with swipe actions (Today, Approvals, Planner) → keep List. The win is that spacing becomes yours rather than negotiated with UIKit.

R10The chore icon is a free-text emoji fieldSmall

ChoreEditorSheet.swift:133 TextField("🧺", text: $icon) · AchievementDefinitionsView.swift:238 TextField("🏆", text: $icon)

Charming, and I'd keep the spirit — emoji as chore icons is warmer than an icon set and lets a family use their own shorthand. But an unbounded text field accepts "asdf", accepts three emoji, accepts nothing, and the result is rendered at 40pt in the kid's list every day.

Proposal. Keep free entry, add a curated first-run grid — the ~24 chores families actually have (🧺 🍽 🐕 🗑 🛏 📚 🦷 🚿 🧹 …) — with the text field as the escape hatch beneath it. Faster for the common case, still open.

R11Completion has one celebration and it's all-or-nothingEmotional

TodayView.swift:187 — confetti gated on store.allDone; ConfettiView.swift is the only celebratory component

Related to R1 but bigger than the control. The app has exactly one reward moment and it requires a perfect day. A kid who does five of six chores gets the same feedback as a kid who does none: nothing. Given the brief — alive and kind in the hand, for a family under strain — that's a lot of unclaimed warmth, and it's the difference between a tracker and a companion.

Proposal. A three-tier ladder, ascending and cheap: per-chore — the R1 check bloom plus haptic; threshold — a quiet flourish when the last earning chore lands or a streak extends, well short of full confetti; perfect day — the existing confetti, now genuinely rarer and therefore worth more. Keep it under the existing enableConfetti setting so a teen can turn the whole ladder down.

R12Ratify the root tint — it's the best decision in the codebaseKeep, document

DailyBreadApp.swift:34 — content.tint(theme.accent(scheme))

Worth calling out because it's easy to mistake for a smell. Roughly 40 call sites use bare Color.accentColor rather than DB.accent(scheme), which looks like theme leakage — it isn't. One .tint() at the root means every downstream control, including system ones, inherits the theme for free. That's why the stock pickers and sliders are at least coloured correctly even where they're shaped wrong.

Proposal. Write it down as the rule, so nobody "fixes" it later: use Color.accentColor for accent; reach for DB.* only for the invariants (gold, help, success, rarity). Same for the graphiteBackground() name — a fossil from the pre-theme era, already flagged in-source as kept for call-site stability. Rename it themeBackground() whenever you next touch those files; it's cosmetic but it misleads every new reader.

This weekendR1 the check button, and R3 the Stepper→pips swap. Both are contained, both are felt daily.
NextR2 duration-first drive logging — the biggest single jump from stock to authored, and the surface a teen judges the app by.
Then, quietlyR5 the fill tokens and R6 rarity invariants — invisible individually, but they stop the drift.
Deliberately not recommended: a custom date picker for the chore date-range (R-none). System DatePicker is correct there — it's a rare, precise, admin-side input, and Apple's is better than anything worth building. Stock isn't the enemy; stock at the emotional centre is.
